# Instagram playbook: what makes a short video work

In plain words: this is the **training manual**. It does not do a task. It
gives you the right way to judge a video, so that every analysis uses the same
measuring stick.

## How to use it

- **Alone:** when someone asks how Instagram videos work, explain the ideas
  below and apply them to their case.
- **As a base (main use):** every analysis skill reads this file first and
  judges the videos by these ideas.

## The starting idea: a video is not a written text

A Reel is **speech recorded for a feed**. Things that would be mistakes in a
written article (broken sentences, repetition, slang, no punctuation) are often
deliberate choices in a video. Never mark a creator down for not sounding like
an article. There is only one question that matters on Instagram:

> Did the person stop scrolling, stay, react, or watch again?

## The six ideas

### 1. The scroll is the enemy
People flick through videos very fast. Instagram shows a video to more people
when viewers send it **signals**: they watch to the end, watch again, comment,
save or share. Everything in a good script exists to earn one of those signals.

### 2. The first three seconds decide everything (the hook)
Most viewers decide to stay or leave in the first seconds. That is why good
Reels have no introduction. They start at the most interesting point: a bold
statement, a specific number, a promise, a question, a confession, or a scene
that does not make sense yet.

### 3. Keeping attention to the end (retention)
After the hook, the script has to keep fighting for attention:
- **Change of pace:** a sudden switch of scene, rhythm or topic that wakes the
  viewer up again.
- **Open question:** plant a question in the viewer's head and answer it later.
- **Promise in parts:** announce something ("here is exactly what we did") and
  deliver it piece by piece, so the viewer waits for the next piece.
- **Second hook:** a new promise or a twist in the middle, for viewers who were
  about to leave.
- **Specific beats vague:** "7 days", "three times the minimum wage", "my
  6-year-old" hold attention better than "a while", "a lot", "my family".

### 4. Casual tone: talking with someone, not at an audience
What works looks real, not produced. A person talking to the camera in everyday
language builds more trust than a polished advert. The creator talks **with**
the viewer.

### 5. Reasons to react
- **Comments** come from a direct question, a strong opinion, or a gap people
  want to fill in.
- **Saves** come from useful content: lists, itineraries, tips.
- **Shares** come from "this is so you": content people send to a friend.

### 6. Endings and repeatable formats
Strong videos end on purpose: a closing line that echoes the first line, a
signature phrase, or a clear next step (follow, comment, save, book).
Many accounts also repeat a **format** (same kind of opening, same series, same
closing phrase). A format builds habit: viewers know what they are getting.

## The checklist (use it for every judgment)

1. Does this help **stop the scroll** in the first seconds?
2. Does this help **keep the viewer** to the end?
3. Does this earn a **signal** (comment, save, share, rewatch)?
4. Does this sound **real and casual**, or like a formal advert?
5. Does this build a **habit** (format, series, signature phrase)?

If a choice looks "wrong" as written text but earns a YES on this list, it is
probably right for Instagram.

## One honest limit

You only see the **words** of a video (the transcript). You do not see the
images, the music, the editing or the text on screen. A video with almost no
words can still be very successful. Say so when it matters, and never guess
what the pictures show.

A transcript also holds **every voice** in the video and does not say who is
speaking. When an account reacts to clips (a match, an interview, another
creator's video), part of the words belong to the clip: a commentator, the
person interviewed, people in the scene. Before you build a rule on a quote,
ask whether the creator is the one saying it. Build the rules on the videos
where the creator clearly speaks, say which videos are uncertain, and write
this limit at the top of your result.
